# Managerial user study

## Purpose

Understand perceived usefulness, trust, explainability, human judgment, fear of errors and organisational barriers when managers review predictive advice. This is a small exploratory qualitative study, not proof of organisation-wide adoption or a causal experiment.

## Sample

The assignment text does not give a minimum sample size. Aim for 8-12 consenting managers, working professionals, team leaders, supervisors, entrepreneurs or other experienced decision-makers, subject to the professor's instructions. This is a proposed convenience/purposive sample; no respondents have been recruited or interviewed by the project code. If the deadline permits fewer, state the achieved number honestly.

## Procedure (10-15 minutes each)

1. Explain the academic purpose and request consent for anonymised analysis and quotations. Do not collect names or employer details in the app. Each session generates a pseudonymous respondent ID.
2. Start directly at Manager study. Ask participants not to explore predictions before recording their initial judgments.
3. Record role, years of experience and decision context.
4. Ask the participant to assign Low, Medium or High retention-review priority to customers A, B and C before seeing model results.
5. Reveal the same three model scores, their explanations, synthetic-data disclosure and performance limitations to each participant. The cases are fixed validation-set examples spanning lower, middle and higher scores.
6. Record final priorities and reasons for keeping or changing each decision. Do not suggest a preferred answer.
7. Collect six 1-5 ratings and nine open-ended answers. Ask neutral follow-ups such as 'Can you give an example?' or 'What makes you say that?' if interviewing live.
8. Have the participant download their response CSV and return it through the researcher's agreed private channel. Confirm receipt. The app does not persist or send responses centrally.
9. Store original response files privately. Keep any contact list separate. Honour withdrawal requests using the respondent ID before submission.

## Ratings

1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree.

- This tool would help me make customer-retention decisions.
- I found the tool easy to understand and use.
- I trust the churn score enough to consider it alongside other evidence.
- The explanation helped me understand the churn score.
- I would consider using a validated version of this tool at work.
- Concern about making a wrong decision would discourage my use.

The last item measures a barrier, so it is not combined with the favourable items into a single score.

## Open-ended questions

1. For which tasks would this tool be useful, and where would it not help?
2. What did you like about the tool?
3. What did you dislike or find confusing?
4. Why did you trust or distrust the prediction? What evidence would increase your trust?
5. If your experience and the model disagree, what would you do, and why?
6. Did seeing the explanation affect your willingness to use the tool? How?
7. What personal or organisational factors would discourage adoption?
8. The viewing-hours model performed worse than a simple average. How should a manager respond?
9. What would need to change before you would use this tool at work?

## Analysis

Upload actual response CSVs under Study analysis. Exact duplicate files are deduplicated; conflicting responses with the same respondent ID require manual resolution. Missing consent, ratings outside 1-5 and invalid priority labels are rejected.

Report sample profile, rating distributions, medians and means with n, plus decision-change counts. These are descriptive summaries of the achieved sample.

Download the coding template and read all responses. Apply one or more codes from the codebook, separated by semicolons. Use OTHER plus an interpretation for unexpected themes. Have a second team member review ambiguous coding if available and record how disagreements were resolved. Do not claim inter-rater reliability unless actually assessed.

For each main theme, report the number of distinct respondents mentioning it, an exact anonymised quotation, an interpretation, a contrasting view if present and a practical design implication. Theme percentages can sum above 100% because a response may have multiple themes.

Changing a judgment after seeing advice does not demonstrate improved accuracy, nor does this protocol isolate the causal effect of explainability. No randomised control or validated real-world outcome is available.
